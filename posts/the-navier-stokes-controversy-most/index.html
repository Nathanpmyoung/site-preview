The Navier-Stokes controversy: Most of what you need to know

A microsite

Some stuff happened about AI solving a Millennium Prize problem ($1m prize). I built a small site to try and pull the facts from the controversy. Here are some prompts with AI responses, in quotes, and then the site. Strikethroughs are egregious mistakes from the AI, since I checked afterwards (using

Prompt: Can you give a layman's description of the controversy?

Two mathematicians, Buckmaster and Alpöge, quietly proved in August that 3D Euler can blow up under a smooth force — the same route Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa opened [fact 1, 47]. Rumours leaked; OpenAI heard them on 1 September, threw ~10,000 agents at the Millennium problems, and on 8 September claimed the full Navier–Stokes version [fact 3, 8]1. On calls that Sunday, Buckmaster says OpenAI pressed him to drop his Anthropic-employed coauthor and dodged his question about training on their Codex drafts; Bubeck denies both readings [fact 19, 18, 26].

Prompt: So what is the Navier-Stokes thing?

The Navier–Stokes equations describe how fluids move — water, air, blood. The Millennium problem asks whether their solutions always stay smooth and finite, or whether a fluid can, mathematically, develop a singularity: some quantity like velocity shooting to infinity in finite time. Fefferman's official statement gives four ways to win, and options (C) and (D) allow you to push the fluid with a smooth external force [fact 4]. Blow-up under a force is easier than the unforced case, which is what everyone pictures when they hear "Navier–Stokes solved".

Prompt: So do you think there was wrongdoing?

On what's checkable: the sharpest allegation is that OpenAI trained on their Codex drafts, and nothing established supports it — OpenAI says no user data was accessed2, Anthropic's Sholto Douglas calls it extremely unlikely [fact 52, 23]. What does look bad is the timing and the ask: OpenAI's first prompt went out only after word of their work reached it [fact 17], and even on Bubeck's own telling he said it would be simpler without an Anthropic employee as author [fact 27]. Rushing to publish ahead of a scooped competitor is sharp practice, not fraud — the "wrongdoing" question is one of norms, and reasonable people split.

Prompt: Any other thoughts?

The thing I’d keep an eye on is the Lean file, not the press. If OpenAI’s formalization typechecks against an honest statement of Theorem 1.1 [fact 53], the priority fight becomes a credit-and-manners dispute rather than a correctness one, and Tao’s line that the forced case was expected and the unforced case is the real prize [fact 10, 12]3 becomes the only mathematically interesting question left. Also worth noting the gap in fact 43: traders put 98% on Navier–Stokes being “considered solved” by June 2027 but only 37 cents on Clay declaring anything by end-2027 — the community and the Institute are measuring different things.

The bottom line

The above drama is not very important. AI has just done work likely sufficient to win a Millennium Prize with a $1 million bounty (though OpenAI are not currently claiming it). This is huge result. What other problems can now be solved by throwing enough computing power at a problem? And the AIs are getting better and better. What problems will the next model be able to solve in drug discovery? What might they accidentally hack next?

But often our attention can be dragged by drama, which is easy to understand, away from the technical details, which are hard. And so, ideally, this site would settle the details so that we can go back to focusing on the maths.
